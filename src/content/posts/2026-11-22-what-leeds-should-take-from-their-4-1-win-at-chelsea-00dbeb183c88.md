At 1–1 at Stamford Bridge, Daniel Farke told Leeds to go for the win. That was the decision worth remembering after the 4–1 scoreline. Leeds had recovered from conceding to Emmanuel Emegha, with Noah Okafor equalising before half-time. Farke saw enough in that first half to ask his side to keep taking the initiative away from home.

Dominic Calvert-Lewin’s 53rd-minute penalty put Leeds ahead. Okafor made it 3–1 five minutes later, and Brenden Aaronson added the fourth in stoppage time. The spell just after the break changed the game. It also changed what each side needed to do. At 1–1, Leeds still had to decide how much risk to take in search of a winner. At 3–1, Chelsea had to chase the game.

**Analysis.** Farke said Leeds had planned to press high and had worked on staying compact as a team. Those two details belong together. A forward can hurry a defender, but the pressure has more value if the midfield is close enough to contest the next pass. For Leeds, the useful question is whether the players can move up together often enough to make that first burst of effort count.

**Counterargument.** There is a case for being selective. An opponent that plays through the first challenge can leave the pressing team running back toward its own goal. Leeds can keep the aggression they showed at Chelsea and still vary when they use it. If the midfield is too far away to follow the forwards, holding their shape may be the better decision.

**Analysis.** The next test for Leeds is whether they can show the same conviction when the goals take longer to come. Farke gave them permission to chase a win with the match level. The part worth repeating is that willingness to take the initiative when the team is close enough together to sustain it.
